# The incident: the owner could not tell that `/retro` had run

**It had run.** The skill was invoked and its body was loaded. What was missing was **the announcement and
the visible steps**.

**The instruction is explicit and was not followed**: say which skill is being used and for what, then
follow its checklist out loud.

## Why this is a failure and not a presentation preference

**A ritual nobody can see running is a ritual nobody can tell was skipped.** From the owner's side — the
only side that matters here — an invoked-but-silent `/retro` and a session that improvised something
retro-shaped are the same observation. And the reason this project has a closing ritual at all is that it
produces what the journal cannot hold ([[the-closing-ritual-produces-what-the-journal-cannot-hold]]); a
step that cannot be observed cannot be relied on to have produced anything.

**It is the same asymmetry the vault applies to its own checks.** A check that prints nothing and a check
that examined nothing look identical, which is why every advisory here prints a line and every coverage
number is stated even at zero ([[an-advisory-prints-one-line-and-sorts-what-it-names]],
[[a-check-that-passed-for-the-wrong-reason-becomes-a-rule]]). **A ritual is a check over a session, and it
owes its output the same way.**

## What it costs, concretely

The retro is where every open question is resolved with the owner, and the questions it surfaces are the
ones the journal has no section for ([[the-retro-step]], [[retro-interrogates-and-handoff-routes]]). A
silent run means **the owner cannot audit which of its steps were taken** — and this session's retro
produced eleven owner decisions, any one of which would have been lost by a step quietly skipped.

**The remedy is a sentence, and the sentence is already written down in the skill.** That is the
uncomfortable part: nothing new has to be designed, and the failure was a step of an existing checklist
being treated as ceremony.
